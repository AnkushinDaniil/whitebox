The [state trie](/how-ethereum-stores-state) is the *what* — the giant key-value map of every account. A **transaction** is the only *how*: the sole mechanism by which an externally-owned account changes that state. Every balance moved, every contract called, every storage slot written starts as a signed message from someone holding a private key.

And here's the thing that surprises people who've only used a wallet UI: a transaction has **no `from` field**. Nowhere in the bytes does it say who sent it. The sender is *derived* — recovered from the signature itself. Let's build one and watch that happen.

<DepthLayer tier="intuition">

## A transaction is a signed instruction — and the signature *is* the identity

Think of a transaction as a check you write and sign. The check says "pay this
account this much, and here's my sequence number so it can't be cashed twice."
But unlike a paper check, you don't write your name on it. The **signature** is
the only proof of who you are — and it's not decorative, it's load-bearing.

A <KeyTerm term="digital signature" def="A value produced from a message + a private key that anyone can verify against the corresponding public key — without the private key. Ethereum uses ECDSA over the secp256k1 curve." /> over
secp256k1 has a special property Ethereum leans on hard: from the signature and
the message, you can **recover the public key** that produced it — and from the
public key, the address. So the network never needs you to *claim* an identity.
It reads the signature, recovers the sender, and that recovered address *is* who
sent it. Claim nothing; prove everything.

That's also why a transaction is tamper-proof in transit. The signature commits
to the exact bytes — recipient, amount, nonce, all of it. Flip a single bit and
the recovered sender becomes a random stranger who never existed. No separate
checksum required; the signature is the integrity check.

</DepthLayer>

A few fields earn their keep:

- **nonce** — your account's transaction counter. It forces strict ordering (tx
  #5 can't land before #4) and makes replay impossible: once nonce 5 is mined,
  any other signed tx with nonce 5 is permanently invalid. Increment-only.
- **value / to / data** — what you're actually doing: move ETH to an address, or
  call a contract (`data` is the calldata). All three are signed over.
- **gas** — `maxFeePerGas` is the most you'll pay per unit of gas; the
  <KeyTerm term="base fee" def="A per-block price, set by the protocol and burned (not paid to the proposer), that rises when blocks are full and falls when they're empty. EIP-1559." /> is
  burned and the rest is the proposer's tip. `gasLimit` caps the work.

<Callout type="insight" title="There is no &quot;from&quot;">
Scroll the transaction's fields again: chainId, nonce, fees, gas, to, value,
data — and the signature. No sender. The network computes `sender =
ecrecover(signingHash, r, s, yParity)`. This is why losing your private key is
fatal and why a valid signature is total authority: identity on Ethereum *is*
the ability to produce a signature that recovers to your address.
</Callout>

</DepthLayer>

## Grounded in the spec

Modern transactions are **typed**. A typed transaction is a single type byte
followed by its payload, so new formats can be added without ambiguity.

<SpecExcerpt
  source="EIP-2718: Typed Transaction Envelope"
  href="https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-2718"
  ref="TransactionType ‖ TransactionPayload"
>
A transaction is `TransactionType || TransactionPayload`. Type `0x02` is the
EIP-1559 transaction, whose payload is the RLP of
`[chainId, nonce, maxPriorityFeePerGas, maxFeePerGas, gasLimit, to, value, data, accessList, yParity, r, s]`.
The thing you sign is `keccak256(0x02 || rlp(everything except yParity, r, s))`.
</SpecExcerpt>

So signing is two steps: deterministically encode the transaction (RLP), hash it
(keccak256), and ECDSA-sign that 32-byte digest. Recovery runs it backward:

<SpecExcerpt
  kind="code"
  source="execution-specs"
  href="https://github.com/ethereum/execution-specs"
  ref="recover_sender"
>
```python
# the sender is not stored — it is recovered
public_key = secp256k1_recover(r, s, y_parity, signing_hash)
sender     = keccak256(public_key)[-20:]   # last 20 bytes of the hash of the pubkey
```
</SpecExcerpt>

<Callout type="note" title="chainId & replay protection">
`chainId` is inside the signed data (EIP-155). That's why a transaction signed
for mainnet can't be replayed on a testnet or an L2 — the signing hash differs,
so it recovers to nobody. Change the chain, change the hash, break the signature.
</Callout>

</DepthLayer>

## Five envelopes, one idea

The typed envelope (EIP-2718) turned "the transaction format" into "a versioned
family." Each type is the same core — signed, nonce-ordered, gas-metered — with
extra fields:

- **`0x00` Legacy** — the original, pre-typed format (still valid).
- **`0x01` EIP-2930** — adds an access list (pre-declared storage, for gas).
- **`0x02` EIP-1559** — the default today: base fee + priority tip.
- **`0x03` EIP-4844** — blob-carrying transactions, the heart of L2 data
  availability (blobs are committed to but not stored in the EVM).
- **`0x04` EIP-7702** — lets an EOA temporarily *act as* a smart-contract
  account for a transaction. Account abstraction, creeping into the EOA.

<Callout type="warning" title="Still moving">
The type list grows as the protocol does. What's invariant is the shape: a type
byte, an RLP payload, and a signature over its keccak256 hash whose recovery
*is* the sender. Hold that and any new type slots in.
</Callout>

Once signed, a transaction doesn't go straight into a block — it enters the
**mempool**, gets gossiped between nodes, and waits for a proposer to pick it up.
That's the next explorable.
