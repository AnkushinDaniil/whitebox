A physical coin can only be in one pocket. A digital coin is just a number — and numbers copy for free. So the founding problem of digital money is **double-spending**: what stops you from paying Alice, then "un-paying" her and spending the same coins on Bob? Solve that without a trusted bank in the middle and you've basically invented a blockchain.

Ethereum stops double-spends with not one defense but **three**, each catching a different version of the attack. Let's name them, then let you try to beat them.

<DepthLayer tier="intuition">

## Two ways to cheat, three ways you're caught

There are two distinct double-spend attacks:

1. **Spend twice from one account.** Sign two transactions that both spend the
   same balance and shove both into the network. The defense is the
   <KeyTerm term="nonce" def="A per-account counter included in every transaction. The protocol only accepts the next expected nonce, so two transactions with the same nonce can never both be valid — the second is rejected." />:
   each transaction carries a sequence number, and the protocol only accepts the
   next one. Two transactions with nonce 7 can't both land — whichever is mined
   first wins, the other becomes permanently invalid. This one's airtight and
   cheap.

2. **Rewrite history.** Pay a merchant, take the goods, then secretly build an
   alternative chain where that payment never happened and convince the network
   it's the "real" one. This is the deep one — it's an attack on *consensus
   itself* — and it's defended by two more layers:
   - **Fork choice** decides which chain is canonical by *weight of votes*, so a
     minority can't simply declare a different history.
   - **Finality** makes blocks past a certain age economically irreversible:
     undoing them would require destroying a third of all the money staked to
     secure the chain.

The merchant's defense against #2 is patience: **wait for finality** and the
payment can't be reversed without someone setting fire to tens of billions of
dollars. Let's make that concrete.

## Try to erase a payment

You control some share of the network's validators. You've just paid a merchant,
received the goods, and now you secretly fork the chain *just before* your
payment, hoping your version — where the payment never happened — becomes
canonical. Drag your validator share up and reveal the attack.

<Callout type="insight" title="The defense is a price, not a wall">
Notice there's no point where the chain says "attack forbidden." Instead, every
threshold has a *cost*. Below ⅓ you achieve nothing. Between ⅓ and ½ you can only
stall — the chain halts rather than tells a lie. Above ½ you can reorg
*unfinalized* blocks, but reversing a *finalized* one still burns ≥⅓ of all
staked ETH (tens of billions of dollars), and acquiring that much stake means
buying it on the open market while knowing it'll be slashed. Ethereum doesn't
make attacks impossible; it makes them irrational.
</Callout>

</DepthLayer>

## Grounded in the spec

The consensus layer combines two algorithms (together called **Gasper**):
**LMD-GHOST** for choosing the head, and **Casper-FFG** for finality.

<SpecExcerpt
  source="consensus-specs"
  href="https://github.com/ethereum/consensus-specs"
  ref="fork choice — get_head"
>
LMD-GHOST (Latest Message Driven, Greediest Heaviest-Observed Sub-Tree): starting
from the last justified checkpoint, repeatedly descend to the child block whose
subtree holds the greatest weight of validators' *latest* attestations, until a
leaf. That leaf is the head. A minority's fork simply never accumulates enough
attestation weight to win.
</SpecExcerpt>

<SpecExcerpt
  source="Casper-FFG + slashing"
  href="https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7251"
  ref="finality & accountable safety"
>
A checkpoint with attestations from ≥⅔ of staked ETH becomes <em>justified</em>;
two justified checkpoints in a row <em>finalize</em> the earlier one. To finalize
two conflicting checkpoints — the only way to revert a finalized block — at least
⅓ of all validators must sign both, which is a provable, <em>slashable</em>
offense. Their entire stake is destroyed. This is "accountable safety": you can't
break finality without identifiable validators losing everything.
</SpecExcerpt>

<Callout type="note" title="And replay attacks">
A subtler cousin: take a transaction signed for one chain and re-broadcast it on
another (or after a fork). Defended by `chainId` being part of the *signed* data
(EIP-155) — and the nonce again. A transaction signed for mainnet recovers to
nobody on an L2 or testnet, because the signing hash differs. One signature, one
chain, one nonce, once.
</Callout>

</DepthLayer>

## The other attacks, briefly

Double-spending is the headline, but it sits in a family of attacks the protocol
prices out:

- **51% / majority** — what you just simulated. Possible in principle, defended
  by stake cost + slashing, not by a wall.
- **Long-range attack** — rewrite ancient history from old keys. Defended by
  <KeyTerm term="weak subjectivity" def="A new or long-offline node trusts a recent finalized checkpoint (from a friend, the client, or a block explorer) as its starting point, rather than trusting the longest chain from genesis. This neutralizes attacks that rewrite distant history." />:
  nodes sync from a recent trusted checkpoint, so deep rewrites are simply
  ignored.
- **Sybil attack** — spin up millions of fake identities to gain influence.
  Defended by Proof of Stake itself: influence is proportional to *staked ETH*,
  not identity count, so fake nodes are free to make and worthless to have.
- **Eclipse attack** — surround a victim node with attacker-controlled peers to
  feed it a false view. Defended at the networking layer (diverse peer selection,
  discv5).
- **Censorship** — refuse to include certain transactions. Mitigated by
  proposer-builder separation and inclusion-list proposals.

The pattern is always the same: identify what the attacker must spend (money,
identities, network position), then make it cost more than the attack can ever
earn.
