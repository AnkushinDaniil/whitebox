The fastest way to understand Ethereum is to *re-invent* it — one fix at a time. Start with the simplest idea that almost works, find the single thing wrong with it, repair exactly that, and continue. Do it eight times and you don't just know the parts; you understand why it couldn't be built any other way.

This is the whole thing, in sequence, in one read. Each step hands you the real, tinker-able tool for that idea before the next problem appears. Follow the rail on the left.

<DepthLayer tier="intuition" id="step-1" num="1" badge="step" title="A shared ledger">

## You and your friends want money without a bank

You're tired of settling cash, so you keep a shared ledger: a public list of who paid whom. "Alice → Bob: 5 Ξ." At the end of the month you tally it and settle up. Trust the ledger, not a bank. Simple — and already broken.

<Callout type="warning" title="The catch">
The ledger is public and editable, so anyone can scribble **"Eve → Eve: 100 Ξ."**
Nothing binds a line to the person it claims to be from. Before anything else, we
need a way to prove a line is genuinely yours.
</Callout>

**→ Step 2: make every line unforgeable.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-2" num="2" badge="step" title="Signatures">

## Sign every line

A <KeyTerm term="private key" def="A secret 256-bit number. Its public key and address are derived from it one-way; only it can produce a valid signature." /> signs each entry. Anyone can verify the signature with your public key, but nobody can forge it — and remarkably, the signer's address can be *recovered* from the signature alone. Your identity simply *is* the ability to sign.

Roll a key, sign a message, and recover the signer. Then tamper with the message and watch the recovered signer turn into a stranger:

<WidgetFrame title="Keypair & signature lab" caption="Private key → public key → address (one-way). Sign, then recover the signer from the signature. Tamper to break it." source="Real secp256k1 ECDSA + EIP-191, validated against viem.">
  <KeyLab client:visible />
</WidgetFrame>

<Callout type="warning" title="The catch">
A signature proves *who* wrote a line — but not that they could afford it, nor
that they didn't sign two lines spending the same money. We have to track what
each account actually holds.
</Callout>

**→ Step 3: give accounts balances.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-3" num="3" badge="step" title="Accounts">

## Track balances, order with nonces

Turn the ledger into a map of `address → balance`. A transaction debits one account and credits another — but only if it's funded — and carries a **nonce**, a per-account counter, so the same payment can't be replayed or slipped in twice. (Ethereum picks accounts over Bitcoin's coins precisely so it can later do more than move money.)

Build and sign a real transaction. Notice it has no "from" field — the sender is *recovered* from the signature, exactly as in step 2:

<WidgetFrame title="Transaction signer" caption="Edit, sign, and recover the sender. Tamper after signing and the signature recovers a stranger." source="Real EIP-1559 + secp256k1, validated against viem.">
  <TxSigner client:visible />
</WidgetFrame>

<Callout type="warning" title="The catch">
Who keeps this map, and whose copy is the truth? If one server hosts it, you've
reinvented the bank you were trying to escape.
</Callout>

**→ Step 4: give everyone a copy.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-4" num="4" badge="step" title="Decentralize">

## Everyone keeps the ledger

So nobody hosts it — *everybody* does. Every node holds the full state and the rules to update it. There's no leader to bribe or shut down; anyone can verify the whole history from genesis. This is the real leap from "a database" to "a blockchain."

<Callout type="warning" title="The catch">
With no leader, whose next block is canonical? Two people can broadcast
conflicting histories — one where you paid the merchant, one where you took the
goods and didn't. That's the **double-spend**, and it's the deepest problem in
the whole design.
</Callout>

**→ Step 5: agree on order, and make lying ruinous.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-5" num="5" badge="step" title="Consensus">

## Make rewriting history ruinously expensive

Validators bond real money — **stake** — to propose and attest to blocks. The chain follows the *heaviest-attested* fork, so a minority can't simply declare a different history. And once a block is **finalized**, reversing it would require validators holding a third of all stake to sign two conflicting versions — a provable, **slashable** offense that destroys their money.

Play the attacker. Take over a share of the validators and try to erase a payment you already made:

<WidgetFrame title="Reorg / double-spend simulator" caption="Set the attacker's stake, reveal the secret fork, and watch fork choice + finality + slashing decide the outcome." source="LMD-GHOST + a Casper-FFG finality model, unit-tested.">
  <ReorgSim client:visible />
</WidgetFrame>

<Callout type="warning" title="The catch">
This is a beautiful way to agree on *payments* — but it's still just moving
balances. I want programmable money: escrows, tokens, auctions, lending. Plain
transfers can't express any of it.
</Callout>

**→ Step 6: put a computer in the ledger.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-6" num="6" badge="step" title="The EVM">

## Put a computer inside the ledger

Let accounts hold **code**, not just a balance. A transaction can call that code, which runs identically on every node — reading and writing state. The ledger becomes a *world computer*, and the programs living in it are smart contracts.

Step through real bytecode and watch the stack, memory, storage, and gas:

<WidgetFrame title="EVM stepper" caption="Step or play through real bytecode. The highlighted op is the program counter; the stack is top-first; gas ticks down per instruction." source="A faithful EVM subset, executed in your browser.">
  <EvmStepper client:visible />
</WidgetFrame>

<Callout type="warning" title="The catch">
A program could loop forever and freeze every node on Earth — the halting
problem, weaponized. You can't run untrusted code for free.
</Callout>

**→ Step 7: charge for every step.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-7" num="7" badge="step" title="Gas">

## Meter every instruction

Each opcode costs **gas**; the transaction prepays a budget; when it runs out, execution halts and reverts. Computation is now bounded and paid for. And the per-block price — the base fee — isn't a blind auction but a *controller* that targets half-full blocks and is **burned**.

Drive demand up and down and watch the base fee respond:

<WidgetFrame title="EIP-1559 base-fee simulator" caption="Set how full each block is. The base fee moves ±12.5%/block toward a 50%-full target, and is burned; the tip goes to the proposer." source="The exact EIP-1559 base-fee formula, unit-tested.">
  <FeeMarket client:visible />
</WidgetFrame>

<Callout type="warning" title="The catch">
Final problem: with all this state — every account, contract, and storage slot —
how does each node cheaply prove it holds the *exact same world* as everyone
else?
</Callout>

**→ Step 8: fold all of state into one number.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-8" num="8" badge="step" title="State root">

## Fold the whole world into one root

A **Merkle-Patricia trie** commits the entire world state to a single 32-byte root, placed in every block header. Match the root and you've proven you hold the identical state — and you can prove any single account with a tiny path, no need to send the rest.

Edit an account and watch the change rewrite the leaf, propagate new hashes up the path, and land a brand-new state root in the header:

<WidgetFrame title="Merkle-Patricia Trie visualizer" caption="Insert or edit an account; the changed path pulses up to a new state root and drops into the block header. Click a node to inspect it." source="A real MPT engine, validated byte-for-byte against @ethereumjs/mpt.">
  <TrieVisualizer client:visible />
</WidgetFrame>

<Callout type="insight" title="And that's Ethereum">
A decentralized, programmable, metered world computer whose entire state is one
verifiable number, secured by staked money. Read back the chain of fixes:
signatures because lines could be forged; balances because signatures don't stop
overspending; decentralization because one host is a bank; consensus because
decentralization invites double-spends; the EVM because payments aren't
programmable; gas because code can loop forever; the state root because nodes
must cheaply agree on the whole world. Nothing was arbitrary — every famous piece
is just the answer to "what broke in the last step?"
</Callout>

</DepthLayer>
