Most explanations of Ethereum hand you the finished machine and name its parts. This one builds it. We start with the dumbest design that almost works, watch the single thing that breaks it, and fix *exactly* that — nothing more. Do it nine times and the famous pieces stop being trivia to memorize. Each one is just the answer to "what broke in the last step?"

Here is the analogy we'll hold the whole way down. Forget coins and miners for a moment. Picture **one spreadsheet that the entire world shares** — a single sheet of who-owns-what that anyone can read and anyone can propose changes to. By the end, that spreadsheet will be able to *run programs*, no one will own it, and lying to it will cost you a fortune. That spreadsheet is Ethereum.

After the nine steps comes the other half: [the real engines](#engines) — a secp256k1 signer, a real Merkle-Patricia trie, a working EVM, the exact EIP-1559 controller, a reorg simulator — each one you can drive yourself, with the spec-level detail behind it.

<DepthLayer tier="intuition" id="step-1" num="1" badge="step" title="The shared sheet">

## One spreadsheet for the whole world

Throw away the bank. In its place keep a single public spreadsheet: one row per account, one column for its balance. To pay someone, you submit an **edit** — "subtract 5 from my row, add 5 to Bob's" — and everyone applies the same edit to their view. There is no statement to request and no teller to ask; the sheet *is* the truth, and the truth is just a pile of numbers that edits move around.

<Scene name="state" caption="The state is a sheet of balances; a transaction is an edit that moves numbers between rows." />

That's the whole mental model of Ethereum's **state**: not a history of payments, but the current value in every row, right now. It's clean, it's shared — and it's wide open.

<Callout type="warning" title="What breaks">
The sheet is public and editable, so nothing stops me from typing **"add 1000 to my own row."** An edit carries no proof of who wrote it. Before anything else, an edit has to be *unforgeable* — provably from the account it claims to touch.
</Callout>

**→ Step 2: make every edit something only its owner could have written.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-2" num="2" badge="step" title="Signing edits">

## A seal only you can stamp

Give every account a secret. Not a password you show to log in — a <KeyTerm term="private key" def="A secret 256-bit number. Its address is derived from it one-way; only the key can produce a valid signature, but anyone can check one." /> that never leaves your hands. With it you press a **seal** onto each edit: a signature. The magic of the seal is asymmetry — producing it requires the secret, but *checking* it requires only your public address. Everyone can confirm the seal is real; no one can counterfeit it.

<Scene name="sign" caption="The signature stamps onto a public message. Anyone verifies it — but change one character and the seal no longer matches." />

Tamper with so much as a single digit of a signed edit and the seal stops matching the contents. The signature isn't glued *near* the message; it's computed *from* it. Your identity on the sheet isn't a name anyone can type — it's simply the ability to produce this stamp.

<Callout type="warning" title="What breaks">
There's a subtlety. The edit still needs a "from" field naming whose row to debit — and a liar could stamp a valid seal of *their own* while pointing the debit at *your* row. The seal must do more than prove authenticity. It has to reveal *who* made it.
</Callout>

**→ Step 3: read the author straight out of the seal.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-3" num="3" badge="step" title="Reading the author">

## The signature already says who you are

Here's the trick that lets Ethereum drop the "from" field entirely. From a signature and the message it covers, you can run the math *backwards* and recover the exact address that must have produced it. The author isn't a claim attached to the edit — it's something you *compute from the seal itself*. Lie about whose row to touch and the recovered address is a stranger with no money to move.

<Scene name="recover" caption="Recovery: the signer's address is derived from the signature alone. The edit never needs to claim who sent it." />

So an Ethereum transaction genuinely has no "from" field. The network takes your signed edit, recovers the address, and *that* is the only row it's allowed to debit. Authorship and authority collapse into one unforgeable stamp — but authorship alone is not yet *safety*.

### A signature is reusable — so the nonce

Here's the flaw the seal doesn't fix: it never expires, and the message it covers is public. The moment you hand Bob a signed "subtract 5 from me, add 5 to Bob," Bob holds a perfectly valid edit he can broadcast *again* — and again. Every copy verifies; every copy moves another 5. One payment becomes a slow leak you never authorized.

<Scene name="replay" caption="A signed message is public and valid forever, so any copy replays. A nonce makes each one single-use." />

The fix is one counter folded into the signed message: a **nonce**, "this is my 7th transaction." The network remembers how many each account has spent and applies only the next number in line. Your nonce-7 edit lands exactly once; every replayed copy now names a 7 the account has already passed, so it bounces. Ordering comes free — an account's edits can only take effect in nonce order, so no one can reshuffle them either.

### Replayable across *worlds* too — so the chainId

One subtler copy remains. The same software runs on testnets and other EVM chains that hold the very same addresses. A signed nonce-7 transfer on a test network would verify just as well *replayed onto the real one*. So the signed message also commits to a **chainId** — making the edit valid on exactly one chain and inert on every other.

A bare transfer is finally watertight, and look at *why* each field is there: a recipient and amount to say what to do, a **nonce** so it can't be replayed here, a **chainId** so it can't be replayed elsewhere, and a signature that *is* the sender. Pull any one out and a specific door swings open.

<Callout type="warning" title="What breaks">
We've been saying "everyone applies the same edit." But *who holds the sheet?* If one server stores it, that server can drop your edits, reorder them, or quietly rewrite a row — and we've reinvented the bank we just fired.
</Callout>

**→ Step 4: let no one hold the master copy.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-4" num="4" badge="step" title="Everyone's copy">

## No master copy — everyone runs the sheet

So nobody hosts the spreadsheet. *Everybody* does. Every participant keeps the full sheet and the rules for updating it, and independently re-checks every signed edit before applying it. There's no privileged server to subpoena, bribe, or switch off. You don't *trust* the network — you keep your own copy and verify it from the very first row.

<Scene name="network" caption="The sheet is replicated everywhere. Each node re-derives the same state from the same edits — trust nobody, verify everything." />

This is the real jump from "a database" to "a blockchain": the data stops living somewhere and starts living *everywhere*, with the rules baked into every copy.

<Callout type="warning" title="What breaks">
Two cracks open at once. Edits arrive at different copies in different *orders*, so whose ordering is the real one? And nothing physically stops me from quietly rewriting last week's edit in my own copy and re-sharing that. Before we can even argue about which history is true, history needs to be **ordered into batches** and made **tamper-evident**.
</Callout>

**→ Step 5: freeze the past into a chain.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-5" num="5" badge="step" title="The blockchain">

## Chain the batches so the past can't move

Stop treating edits as loose slips of paper. Gather them into a numbered **block** and reduce it to a small **header**: its number, the **parentHash** (the hash of the block before it), a **txsRoot** fingerprinting the edits inside, and a **stateRoot** — a single fingerprint of the *entire* sheet, which we build in step 9 as a tree of hashes (a trie). The block's own **hash** is computed from that whole header. A hash changes completely if even one bit of its input changes, so the header binds the block to all of history before it *and* to the exact state it produced.

<Scene name="blocks" wide caption="A full state trie of four accounts under the block header. Edit Alice's balance (14 → 9) and watch the actual recompute: her leaf hash changes (3c1a → 8a47), its branch re-hashes (3c08 → 8a41), the root re-hashes (91f3 → 2d7a) into a new stateRoot (9f12 → a2e0), the block hash changes, and every later block breaks — while Bob, Carol, and Dave's untouched subtrees keep their hashes." />

Now watch what one tampered balance does. Change `Alice 14 → 9` deep in block #1's state and the effect doesn't stay local. Her account's **leaf** changes, which re-hashes up the trie to a new **stateRoot**; the stateRoot sits in block #1's **header**, so the header changes; the header's **hash** changes; and now block #2's **parentHash** points at a hash that no longer exists — so block #2 is invalid, which invalidates block #3, and so on down the line. One edited number, and the *entire* chain after it falls apart. To rewrite a single past balance you'd have to silently rebuild every block since. The past is frozen not by a guard but by the sheer weight of everything stacked on top. That linked, batched, state-committed history is the **blockchain**.

<Callout type="warning" title="What breaks">
Hash-linking makes *rewriting* a chain obvious — but it doesn't say which chain is the real one. Anyone can build a perfectly valid chain. I can secretly grow an alternate one where I never paid the merchant and reveal it later. Both are valid blockchains. Whose is canonical? This is the **double-spend**, the hardest problem in the design.
</Callout>

**→ Step 6: agree on one chain, and make lying self-destructive.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-6" num="6" badge="step" title="Agreeing on one chain">

## Agree on one history — and price the lie

We can't appoint a referee, so the network has to *converge* on one chain by itself. The obvious idea — let everyone vote, one node one vote — dies instantly. Identities are free: I spin up a million fake nodes (a **Sybil attack**) and outvote the honest world before breakfast.

<Scene name="sybil" caption="Votes counted per identity fail — identities are free to forge. A vote has to cost something real you can't fake." />

So a vote must *cost* something unforgeable. There are two famous prices. **Proof of work** charges electricity: to extend the chain you burn real computation, and the chain with the most work behind it wins, because faking it means out-burning the entire honest network. **Proof of stake** — Ethereum's choice — charges *bonded money*: validators lock up a deposit to propose and attest to blocks. It's cheaper and greener, but the real prize is something work can't offer: because every voter is a known, bonded deposit, a liar can be *punished*, not merely out-competed.

On that base, two mechanisms settle the chain. **Fork choice** keeps the network on one history moment to moment: follow the fork with the most attesting stake behind it, so a minority can never make their branch the heaviest. **Finality** makes old history permanent: periodically validators vote to *finalize* a checkpoint, and once two-thirds of all stake has, reversing it would require a third of all stake to sign two contradictory checkpoints — a provable crime the protocol catches and punishes by **slashing**, destroying their deposits.

<Scene name="consensus" caption="The chain follows the most-staked fork; a finalized block can't be reversed without validators burning a third of all stake." />

So the attacker's own deposit is the deterrent. The double-spend isn't stopped by a guard at the door — it's stopped because pulling it off at scale sets fire to a fortune you posted in advance, while honest validators simply keep the heaviest, finalized chain.

<Callout type="warning" title="What breaks">
This is a beautiful way to agree on *balances*. But all we can express is "move money from row A to row B." I want escrows, tokens, auctions, votes, loans — *rules*, not just transfers. A column of numbers can't hold a rule.
</Callout>

**→ Step 7: let the cells hold programs.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-7" num="7" badge="step" title="Cells that compute">

## Cells that run programs

Upgrade the sheet one more time: let a row hold not just a balance but **code**. Now an edit can *call* that code, which runs against the shared state — reading rows, writing rows, refusing if its rules aren't met. Because every node runs the identical program over the identical state, they all reach the identical result. The spreadsheet has quietly become a **world computer**, and the little programs living in its cells are smart contracts.

<Scene name="evm" caption="A cell holds a tiny program. A virtual machine steps through its instructions over a stack — the same steps, same result, on every node on Earth." />

This is the leap that makes Ethereum *Ethereum* rather than a faster bank. A contract is just an account whose behavior is fixed in code that anyone can read and no one can secretly override — an escrow that releases when conditions are met, a token whose supply rules are public, a vote no clerk can miscount.

And it forces one more field onto the transaction. To *call* a contract, an edit has to say which function and with what arguments — that payload is the **data** field. A plain transfer leaves it empty; calling code fills it with the encoded call. (If a transaction has no recipient at all, the data field is read as the code for a *brand-new* contract — which is exactly how contracts get deployed.)

<Callout type="warning" title="What breaks">
Programs can loop forever. One malicious cell saying `while (true) {}` would freeze every node on the planet trying to run it — the halting problem, turned into a weapon. You cannot run untrusted code for free.
</Callout>

**→ Step 8: put a meter on every instruction.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-8" num="8" badge="step" title="Metering work">

## A meter so nothing runs forever

Charge for computation, one instruction at a time. Every operation has a price in **gas**; each transaction prepays a gas budget; the meter ticks down as the program runs, and the instant it hits zero, execution halts and the changes revert. An infinite loop no longer freezes the world — it simply runs out of fuel and dies, with the attacker having paid for every step. Pricing *is* the halting solution.

<Scene name="gas" caption="Each instruction spends gas from a prepaid budget. Run out and execution halts — so no program can run forever." />

That prepaid budget is itself a transaction field — the **gasLimit** — and it quietly does a second job: since execution can never burn more gas than the limit, it caps your worst-case loss when you call a contract whose behavior you don't fully trust. The price you pay needs fields too. Block-to-block the base fee swings, so you don't name an exact price; you name a ceiling, **maxFeePerGas** (the most you'll tolerate), and a **maxPriorityFeePerGas** — a tip on top of the burned base fee to give the proposer a reason to include you. The base fee itself isn't a blind auction; it's a controller that drifts up when blocks are full and down when they're empty, targeting half-full blocks, and it's **burned** rather than pocketed.

That completes the transaction. Lay it out and not one field is decoration — each is the answer to a specific *"what breaks without it?"*

<Scene name="txfields" caption="The whole transaction, field by field. Each row is forced by the attack or failure it prevents — nothing is arbitrary." />

<Callout type="warning" title="What breaks">
One last gap. Every node is supposed to hold the *exact same* world — every row, every contract, every storage slot. How does a node prove, cheaply and instantly, that its copy matches everyone else's, without shipping the entire spreadsheet to compare?
</Callout>

**→ Step 9: fold the whole world into one number.**

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="mechanism" id="step-9" num="9" badge="step" title="One fingerprint">

## One fingerprint for the entire world

Back to the catch: every node must check, cheaply, that it holds the identical world. The naive fix is to hash the entire sheet into one number and compare. It half-works and breaks in two ways: changing a *single* balance forces you to re-hash the whole sheet, and to prove your balance to someone you'd have to hand them the *entire* sheet so they could re-hash it.

Fix both with a **tree of hashes**. Hash accounts in groups, then hash those hashes, level by level, up to a single root. Now editing one account only re-hashes the short path from its leaf to the root — not the whole sheet — and you can prove one account to someone holding only the root by sending just the sibling hashes along that path. That's a Merkle tree.

But state isn't a tidy fixed array; it's a sparse, ever-changing map keyed by 20-byte addresses. A plain tree doesn't encode *where* an account lives. So make the key *be* the path: read the address as a string of hex digits — **nibbles** — and walk them down the tree to find the account.

<Scene name="triepath" caption="An address is a path of nibbles. Walk it down the tree to the account; three node types make that walk efficient." />

Walking that path efficiently forces exactly three kinds of node, and each earns its place. A **branch** node is a 16-way fork — one slot per possible next nibble — used wherever paths diverge. A **leaf** holds the final stretch of path and the account's value. And an **extension** node compresses a long run of nibbles that everything below happens to share, so the tree doesn't waste a whole 16-way branch on a stretch where nothing forks. Hash every node from its children — keccak of its encoding — up to one root, and you have Ethereum's **Merkle-Patricia trie**: the Patricia part makes keyed updates cheap, the Merkle part makes the whole thing one verifiable fingerprint.

<Scene name="root" caption="Edit an account, re-hash up its path, and a brand-new 32-byte state root lands in the block header — the number every node compares." />

<Callout type="insight" title="And that is Ethereum">
A programmable, decentralized, metered world computer, secured by staked money, whose entire state collapses to one verifiable number. Read the chain of fixes backward and nothing in it was arbitrary: **signatures** because edits could be forged; **recovery** because authorship had to be provable, not claimed; the **nonce** and **chainId** because a signature alone is endlessly replayable; **decentralization** because one host is a bank; the **hash-linked blockchain** because a copied ledger needs an ordered, tamper-evident past; **proof-of-stake consensus** because forks need a costly, punishable vote to converge on one chain; the **EVM** and its **data** field because numbers can't hold rules; **gas** with its **limit** and **fee** fields because code can loop forever and must be paid for; the **Merkle-Patricia trie** and its **state root** because nodes must agree on the whole world at a glance — and prove any part of it cheaply. Not one field, not one mechanism, was a free choice — each was forced by the flaw in the one before it. You could have invented every piece.
</Callout>

</DepthLayer>

## Roll a key. Sign. Recover.

The seal from step 2 is **secp256k1 ECDSA**. Watch the one-way pipeline — private key → public key → address — then sign a message and recover the signer from the signature *alone*. Edit the message after signing and the recovered signer turns into a stranger.

### Signing is not encryption

The single most common misconception. The same keypair *could* encrypt — but with the roles reversed, and Ethereum L1 doesn't encrypt your transactions at all:

|  | Signing (what Ethereum uses) | Encryption (what it doesn't) |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | authenticity + integrity | confidentiality |
| Private key | **signs** | decrypts |
| Public key | **verifies / recovers** | encrypts |
| The message is | **public** | hidden |

Your transaction is plaintext in the mempool and on-chain forever. Privacy is a separate, harder problem (zk-proofs, mixers) — *not* a property of the base signing scheme.

<SpecExcerpt
  kind="code"
  source="execution-specs"
  href="https://github.com/ethereum/execution-specs"
  ref="address derivation & recovery"
>
```python
public_key = secp256k1_point(private_key)        # 64 bytes: (x, y) on the curve
address    = keccak256(public_key)[-20:]          # last 20 bytes of the hash
# signing a message (EIP-191 / personal_sign):
digest = keccak256(b"\x19Ethereum Signed Message:\n" + str(len(msg)) + msg)
r, s, y_parity = ecdsa_sign(digest, private_key)
signer  = keccak256(secp256k1_recover(r, s, y_parity, digest))[-20:]
```
</SpecExcerpt>

<Callout type="note" title="Why the \x19 prefix?">
The `\x19Ethereum Signed Message:` prefix (EIP-191) exists so a signed *message*
can never be mistaken for a signed *transaction* — otherwise a dapp could trick
you into signing bytes that happen to be a valid transaction draining your
account. Always sign into a clearly-labelled context.
</Callout>

</DepthLayer>

## Build one. Sign it. Recover the sender.

A real EIP-1559 transaction and signer — the same encoding and curve a live wallet uses. Edit the fields, sign, and watch the sender get *recovered* from the signature (it never appears in the transaction). Tamper after signing and recovery falls apart.

<WidgetFrame

  source="Real secp256k1 ECDSA + EIP-1559 RLP; signing hash, tx hash, and recovery validated byte-for-byte against viem."
>
  <TxSigner client:visible />
</WidgetFrame>

Modern transactions are **typed** — a type byte followed by an RLP payload, so new formats slot in without ambiguity:

<SpecExcerpt
  source="EIP-2718: Typed Transaction Envelope"
  href="https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-2718"
  ref="TransactionType ‖ TransactionPayload"
>
A transaction is `TransactionType || TransactionPayload`. Type `0x02` is the
EIP-1559 transaction, whose payload is the RLP of
`[chainId, nonce, maxPriorityFeePerGas, maxFeePerGas, gasLimit, to, value, data, accessList, yParity, r, s]`.
The thing you sign is `keccak256(0x02 || rlp(everything except yParity, r, s))`.
</SpecExcerpt>

That envelope turned "the transaction format" into a versioned family — same core (signed, nonce-ordered, gas-metered), extra fields:

- **`0x00` Legacy** — the original, pre-typed format.
- **`0x01` EIP-2930** — adds an access list (pre-declared storage, for gas).
- **`0x02` EIP-1559** — the default: base fee + priority tip.
- **`0x03` EIP-4844** — blob-carrying transactions, the heart of L2 data availability.
- **`0x04` EIP-7702** — lets an EOA temporarily *act as* a smart-contract account.

<Callout type="note" title="chainId & replay protection">
`chainId` lives *inside* the signed data (EIP-155), so a transaction signed for
mainnet recovers to nobody on a testnet or L2 — the signing hash differs. One
signature, one chain, one nonce, once.
</Callout>

<SpecExcerpt
  source="Ethereum Yellow Paper"
  href="https://ethereum.github.io/yellowpaper/paper.pdf"
  ref="Appendix D, Modified Merkle-Patricia Tree"
>
The trie's node-cap function hashes a node's RLP only when it is at least 32
bytes; shorter nodes are **inlined** directly into their parent. That one rule —
embed if `len(rlp) < 32`, else reference by `keccak256(rlp)` — is the single most
common reason a hand-rolled trie's root fails to match a client's.
</SpecExcerpt>

For the **state** trie the key is `keccak256(address)` and the leaf value is the RLP of the account's four fields:

<SpecExcerpt
  kind="code"
  source="execution-specs"
  href="https://github.com/ethereum/execution-specs"
  ref="state account"
>
```python
class Account:
    nonce: Uint           # transactions sent from this account
    balance: U256         # wei
    storage_root: Root    # root of THIS account's storage trie (tries nest!)
    code_hash: Hash32     # keccak256 of contract code (empty-code hash for EOAs)
```
</SpecExcerpt>

Note `storage_root`: each contract holds *another* MPT for its storage, whose root is a field inside the account leaf — so a single storage write ripples up a storage root into the account leaf, then a state root into the header. The same ripple from step 9, nested one level deeper.

### The witness problem, and the road past the MPT

To **prove** one account without holding all of state — what a <KeyTerm term="stateless client" def="A node that verifies blocks using witnesses (proofs) supplied with the block, instead of storing the full state itself." /> needs — you must reveal, at every level, the *sibling* hashes you didn't descend into. A 16-way branch means up to 15 siblings per level; multiplied by depth, witnesses balloon into megabytes. That's the roadblock to statelessness.

<WidgetFrame
  title="Hexary MPT → Verkle → Binary"
  caption="The same account, proven three ways. Watch how node arity and witness size trade off — and why the roadmap moved."
>
  <StructureCompare client:visible />
</WidgetFrame>

For years the fix was **Verkle trees** (vector commitments: one short proof for all 256 children, siblings stop mattering). Through 2024–2025 the roadmap pivoted toward a **binary Merkle tree** (EIP-7864): arity 2, ordinary hashing, no trusted setup, and friendly to the <KeyTerm term="SNARK provers" def="Systems that produce succinct proofs that a computation was done correctly. Simple hash-based trees are far cheaper to prove inside a SNARK than vector-commitment schemes." /> that will eventually verify Ethereum's state transitions.

<Callout type="warning" title="Moving target">
Live protocol design, not settled history. Verkle was the plan; binary trees
(EIP-7864) are the current front-runner. The durable lesson is the chain of
cause: **commitment scheme → tree shape → witness size.**
</Callout>

</DepthLayer>

<Callout type="insight" title="Storage is the expensive resource">
Watch the gas column. `ADD`, `PUSH`, `MLOAD` cost 3; `SSTORE` of a fresh slot
costs **20,000**. Memory vanishes when the call ends, but every storage slot must
be replicated and retained by every node forever — gas is a mirror of real,
lasting resource use. "Gas golfing" is mostly a fight to touch storage less.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Why JUMPs need JUMPDEST">
A jump target must be a `JUMPDEST` (0x5b) opcode — and not a byte that happens to
sit *inside* a `PUSH`'s immediate data. That jumpdest analysis keeps control flow
analyzable and stops an attacker from jumping into the middle of an instruction.
</Callout>

Everything on the stack is a **256-bit** word — sized to hold a keccak256 hash or an address, with arithmetic wrapping modulo 2²⁵⁶ (try `0 - 1` for the max uint256). And the abstractions you write compile straight to this: a `mapping` is a `keccak256(key . slot)` storage address; a function call dispatches on the first 4 bytes of calldata (the <KeyTerm term="function selector" def="The first 4 bytes of keccak256 of the function signature. The contract's dispatcher compares calldata's first 4 bytes against these to pick which function runs." />); `revert` undoes state and refunds remaining gas.

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="spec" id="lab-fees" title="The fee market & the mempool (step 8)">

## Drive the thermostat

A signed transaction first enters the **mempool** — a loosely-shared, per-node waiting room, gossiped peer to peer until a proposer includes it. There it's priced by the EIP-1559 controller. Set how full each block is and watch the base fee respond: full blocks push it up (≤12.5%/block), empty blocks pull it down, targeting half-full blocks.

<WidgetFrame
  title="EIP-1559 base-fee simulator"
  caption="Click a block to change how full it is (or pick a demand pattern). The base fee runs forward block by block — up over target, down under, ±12.5% max. The base fee is burned; the tip goes to the proposer."
  source="The exact EIP-1559 base-fee formula, in integer arithmetic — unit-tested against the spec."
>
  <FeeMarket client:visible />
</WidgetFrame>

<SpecExcerpt
  source="EIP-1559: Fee market change"
  href="https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-1559"
  ref="base fee calculation"
>
```python
# parent over target → base fee rises (capped at +1/8); under → falls
if parent_gas_used == parent_gas_target:
    base_fee = parent_base_fee
elif parent_gas_used > parent_gas_target:
    delta   = parent_base_fee * (parent_gas_used - parent_gas_target) \
              // parent_gas_target // BASE_FEE_MAX_CHANGE_DENOMINATOR   # = 8
    base_fee = parent_base_fee + max(delta, 1)
else:
    delta   = parent_base_fee * (parent_gas_target - parent_gas_used) \
              // parent_gas_target // BASE_FEE_MAX_CHANGE_DENOMINATOR
    base_fee = parent_base_fee - delta
```
</SpecExcerpt>

What a transaction pays per gas is `min(maxFeePerGas, baseFee + maxPriorityFeePerGas)`. If the base fee climbs above your `maxFeePerGas`, the transaction simply waits in the mempool rather than overpaying.

### The tip is for ordering — and that opens a market

The base fee gets you *includable*; the tip competes for *position*, and position has value (a liquidation to capture, an arbitrage to front-run). That value is <KeyTerm term="MEV" def="Maximal Extractable Value — the profit a builder can extract by choosing which transactions to include and in what order. It turned block-building into a competitive market." />. Under **proposer-builder separation (PBS)**, specialized builders assemble the most valuable block they can and bid for the proposer to publish it; the proposer just picks the highest bid.

</DepthLayer>

<DepthLayer tier="spec" id="lab-consensus" title="Consensus & the threat model (step 6)">

## Play the attacker

You control a share of the validators. You've paid a merchant, taken the goods, and now secretly fork the chain just before your payment, hoping your version wins. Drag your stake up and reveal the attack.

<WidgetFrame
  title="Reorg / double-spend simulator"
  caption="Set how much stake the attacker controls, then reveal the secret fork. Fork choice picks the heavier chain; finality locks blocks; the verdict shows whether the double-spend works — and what it would cost."
  source="LMD-GHOST head selection + a Casper-FFG finality model, unit-tested. Stake/cost figures are real-order-of-magnitude."
>
  <ReorgSim client:visible />
</WidgetFrame>

The consensus layer is two algorithms together called **Gasper**:

<SpecExcerpt
  source="consensus-specs"
  href="https://github.com/ethereum/consensus-specs"
  ref="fork choice — get_head"
>
LMD-GHOST (Latest Message Driven, Greediest Heaviest-Observed Sub-Tree): from the
last justified checkpoint, repeatedly descend to the child whose subtree holds the
greatest weight of validators' *latest* attestations, until a leaf. That leaf is
the head — a minority's fork never accumulates enough weight to win.
</SpecExcerpt>

<SpecExcerpt
  source="Casper-FFG + slashing"
  href="https://github.com/ethereum/consensus-specs"
  ref="finality & accountable safety"
>
A checkpoint with attestations from ≥⅔ of staked ETH becomes <em>justified</em>;
two justified in a row <em>finalize</em> the earlier one. To revert a finalized
block, ≥⅓ of all validators must sign two conflicting checkpoints — a provable,
<em>slashable</em> offense that destroys their entire stake. That's "accountable
safety": you can't break finality without identifiable validators losing everything.
</SpecExcerpt>

<Callout type="insight" title="The defense is a price, not a wall">
There's no point where the chain says "attack forbidden." Below ⅓ you achieve
nothing; between ⅓ and ½ you can only *stall* (the chain halts rather than lies);
above ½ you can reorg *unfinalized* blocks, but reversing a *finalized* one burns
≥⅓ of all staked ETH. Ethereum doesn't make attacks impossible — it makes them irrational.
</Callout>

Double-spending sits in a family the protocol prices out: **51%** (stake cost + slashing), **long-range** rewrites of ancient history (defended by <KeyTerm term="weak subjectivity" def="A new or long-offline node trusts a recent finalized checkpoint as its starting point, rather than the longest chain from genesis — neutralizing rewrites of distant history." />), **Sybil** (influence ∝ staked ETH, not identity count), **eclipse** (diverse peer selection at the networking layer), and **censorship** (mitigated by PBS and inclusion lists). The pattern never changes: find what the attacker must spend, and make it cost more than the attack can earn.

</DepthLayer>
