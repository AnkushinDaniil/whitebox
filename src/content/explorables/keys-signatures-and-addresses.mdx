Before state, before transactions, before consensus, there's one primitive everything else rests on: a way to prove *"I authorized this"* to people who don't trust you and never will — without handing over any secret. That's **asymmetric cryptography**, and getting it precisely right is what separates understanding Ethereum from waving at it.

The headline, stated carefully because almost everyone gets it slightly wrong: Ethereum uses public-key crypto for **signatures** — *authenticity and integrity* — **not encryption**. Your transactions are public, readable by anyone. The magic isn't that nobody can *see* what you did; it's that nobody can *forge* it, and everyone can independently *verify* it.

<DepthLayer tier="intuition">

## One secret, two superpowers

Pick a 256-bit random number. That's your <KeyTerm term="private key" def="A secret 256-bit integer. Everything else — public key, address, signatures — is derived from it. Whoever knows it controls the account; there is no recovery." />. From
it, elliptic-curve math derives a **public key**, and hashing the public key
gives your **address**. The arrows only run one way: trivial to go
private → public → address, computationally impossible to go back.

Now the two superpowers of that secret:

- **Sign:** combine your private key with a message to produce a *signature*.
  Anyone holding your public key (or just the signature) can confirm it came
  from you — yet the signature reveals nothing about the private key.
- **Recover:** Ethereum goes one better than "verify." From a message and its
  signature, you can mathematically **recover the public key** that made it — and
  thus the address. No identity needs to be claimed; it's reconstructed from the
  signature itself.

That second property is why an Ethereum transaction has no "from" field, why an
address *is* an identity, and why losing the private key is final. The signature
isn't attached to the authorization — it *is* the authorization.

</DepthLayer>

<Callout type="insight" title="Verifying needs no secret">
Notice what recovery *didn't* need: your private key. Anyone — every node on the
network — can take the message and signature and recover your address, with only
public information. That asymmetry (secret to sign, public to verify) is the
entire foundation of trustless systems. You prove authority without ever
exposing the thing that grants it.

## Grounded in the spec

The curve is **secp256k1**; the signature scheme is **ECDSA**, producing two
field elements `(r, s)` plus a 1-bit recovery id (`yParity`, surfaced as `v`).
The address is the low 20 bytes of the keccak256 of the 64-byte public key.

<SpecExcerpt
  kind="code"
  source="execution-specs"
  href="https://github.com/ethereum/execution-specs"
  ref="address derivation"
>
```python
public_key = secp256k1_point(private_key)        # 64 bytes: (x, y) on the curve
address    = keccak256(public_key)[-20:]          # last 20 bytes of the hash
# signing a message (EIP-191 / personal_sign):
digest = keccak256(b"\x19Ethereum Signed Message:\n" + str(len(msg)) + msg)
r, s, y_parity = ecdsa_sign(digest, private_key)
signer  = keccak256(secp256k1_recover(r, s, y_parity, digest))[-20:]
```
</SpecExcerpt>

<Callout type="note" title="Why the \x19 prefix?">
The `\x19Ethereum Signed Message:` prefix (EIP-191) exists so a signed *message*
can never be mistaken for a signed *transaction*. Without it, a dapp could trick
you into signing bytes that happen to be a valid transaction draining your
account. Domain separation: always sign into a clearly-labelled context.
</Callout>

## Signing is not encryption

This is the distinction worth burning in. The same keypair *could* be used for
encryption — but with the roles reversed, and Ethereum L1 doesn't do it to your
transactions:

|  | Signing (what Ethereum uses) | Encryption (what it doesn't) |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | authenticity + integrity | confidentiality |
| Private key | **signs** | decrypts |
| Public key | **verifies / recovers** | encrypts |
| The message is | **public** | hidden |

Your transaction is plaintext in the mempool and on-chain forever. Privacy on
Ethereum is a separate, harder problem (zk-proofs, mixers) — *not* a property of
the base signing scheme.
